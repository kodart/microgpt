microgpt · Rust port · Apple M1 Pro · September 2026

The gist's GPT, 8,000x faster, and what that speed let us learn

Karpathy's microgpt.py trains a 4,192-parameter character-level GPT on 32,033 names with a scalar autograd engine. This port keeps the maths and drops the engine. The wall-clock savings were then spent on the sweeps below: batch size, threads, learning rate, seeds and model size, each judged on the same 1,000 held-out names, and finally on a mixture-of-experts MLP whose experts turn out to be readable.

01 · What changed

Same maths, no graph

The Python version allocates a Value node for every add and multiply and walks that graph backwards; that is essentially all of its runtime. The port derives the backward pass by hand for each op and runs it as loops over flat f32 buffers. Everything else is bookkeeping around that. Each row below was measured in isolation, in the order it was added.

ChangeWhat it doesMeasured effect
Hand-derived vector backwardExplicit backward kernels for embedding, rmsnorm, fused QKV, causal softmax attention, output projection, ReLU MLP, LM head and cross-entropy. Parameters, gradients and Adam moments are four flat vectors.109 s → 20 ms
Fixed-size activation arraysActivations and backward scratch are inline arrays in one boxed struct instead of separate heap Vecs. With Vecs reached through a reference, LLVM cannot prove the buffers are disjoint and emits ~25% fewer vector ops plus spills. This cost a day's worth of bisecting to find.15.9 → 12.5 µs per name
Vector FMA kernelsEvery accumulate uses mul_add; the dot product keeps four independent lanes. Rust neither contracts a*b+c nor reassociates float sums, so the hot loop had 602 multiplies and 600 adds and now has 568 fused multiply-adds. A serial FMA chain was slower (latency-bound); the lanes are what matters.12.2 → 10.6 µs per name
Position-batched linear layersForward and backward apply each linear layer to all positions of a name at once. The weight gradient becomes dW += dY^T X summed over positions with 16-float chunks of each output row held in registers, so dW is touched once per name instead of once per position.10.6 → 9.1 µs per name
Forward against transposed weightsA profile (never-inlined kernels under sample) showed the forward's one-dot-per-output form taking 45% of the time for a third of the FLOPs: a 16-element dot is mostly horizontal-reduction overhead. The forward now reads a transposed copy of the weights, refreshed after each Adam step in about a microsecond, and computes each output row as register-accumulated y += x[j]·Wt[j] updates, the same shape as the backward matmuls. Two earlier guesses, a fast exp and a 4-row tiled dot, measured no gain and a loss respectively; the profile was what found this.9.1 → 7.3 µs per name
Minibatches over a worker poolWorkers claim names from an atomic counter and accumulate into private gradient buffers; the main thread reduces, runs Adam and publishes the next step on an atomic epoch. Spin-then-yield waits, because a step is tens of microseconds and channel wake-ups cost more than the compute.2.8x at 4 threads, 5x at 8
Reduce-and-Adam fused into the workersThe per-step tail (sum the gradient buffers, Adam, transpose) ran on the main thread while the others spun. Each worker now does it for its own parameter slice behind a second barrier, on lock-free shared buffers whose soundness rests on the barriers. Instrumenting the phases was essential: a first version was 3x slower per element than the old serial tail because its chunked reduce ran the vector sqrt/div latency-bound and its index-table scatter defeated the store pipeline; flat loops and a strided transpose fixed both.46.8 → 43.8 µs per step at 4 threads; 63 → 58 at 8
Build flagsFat LTO, one codegen unit, panic=abort, target-cpu=native.baseline

Per-name costs are single-thread, 32 names per step; the port now trains the gist's model at about 7.5 µs per name, against roughly 100 ms per name in Python. The KV cache for sampling comes for free: the stored K/V rows of the activation buffer are the cache, and generation runs one new position against them. The 4-thread speed-up fell from 3.2x to 2.6x as the kernels got faster, because the per-step synchronisation cost did not shrink with them.

02 · Threads

Scaling flattens early, and eight threads are fragile

Time per optimizer step, 32 names per step

Quiet-machine measurements with the final code, 20000 steps. Ten-thread runs are omitted: on this 8-performance + 2-efficiency-core CPU they were 1.6 to 3x slower than eight and never repeatable.

Table view

A 32-name step is about 30 µs of arithmetic on eight cores; the two barriers, the parallel update and the load imbalance between names of different lengths add another 25 µs that does not shrink with threads. That fixed cost is why the curve bends. It is also why eight threads are a bad default: with all performance cores busy, any other process (a browser, or macOS's Gatekeeper scanning freshly built binaries, which happened repeatedly during this work) turned 1.5 s runs into 3 to 8 s, while 4-thread runs stayed at 1.1 s throughout. Six threads are about 20% faster than four on a quiet machine and are worth passing explicitly.

Two results that went against the usual intuition. At equal data, larger batches win: the one-name-per-step recipe updates most often but its gradients are so noisy that the extra updates are worth less than the noise costs, and the trend only flattens at 64. And larger batches do not want a larger learning rate: 0.01 is already the optimum for 16- and 64-name batches, with every higher rate strictly worse. The batched runs are noise-limited, not step-limited. The one recipe that does want a change is the gist's own, which improves from 2.223 to 2.203 at a rate of 0.005.

The shape of the decay was tested too, with a MICROGPT_SCHEDULE knob. Linear and cosine decay to zero are indistinguishable at the default run over three seeds (2.121-2.130 against 2.127-2.132), and cosine leads by 0.003-0.006 in single-seed 64 x 4 runs, dense and 8-expert alike, which is within noise. A constant rate with no decay costs 0.07. So the annealing to zero is what matters, not its shape, and linear stays the default.

Four seeds of the default run end at 2.120, 2.122, 2.123 and 2.130, and re-running one configuration moves it by up to 0.007 through thread summation order alone. Differences under about 0.01 in any table here are noise; the batch-size and model-size effects are several times that.

06 · Mixture of experts

More parameters per token of compute

The MLP of the block can be a mixture of experts, the structure GLM, DeepSeek and Mixtral use: several MLPs ("experts") and a router, a tiny N_EXPERTS x 16 matrix, that scores them per position. The top-k experts run, their outputs are mixed with the renormalised router probabilities as gates, and the sum joins the residual stream. Parameters grow with the expert count; compute per token grows only with k x width. The dense model is the one-expert, top-1 case and is bit-for-bit unchanged.

Implementation: the (position, slot) pairs of a name are grouped by expert, each expert's inputs are gathered into a contiguous buffer, and every expert runs as one position-batched matmul with the same kernels as the dense path. The backward pass mirrors it: chosen experts get gradients scaled by their gate, the gate gradient dx · expert_output flows through the renormalisation and softmax into the router, unchosen experts get nothing. Because unchosen experts never learn, a per-name balancing loss (Switch Transformer style, coefficient 0.01) pushes the router away from experts it overuses. The f64 gradient check covers router and experts, skipping parameters whose perturbation flips a top-k choice.

Three readings. Top-2 at the dense model's per-token compute (4 experts of 32) already beats it by 0.01 to 0.015, above the noise floor. More and wider experts keep helping when there is data to feed them: 8 experts of 64 at 64 x 4 reaches 2.049 in 5.6 s, better and faster than the 32-dim dense single layer (2.053 in 7.9 s), which is the whole promise of the architecture in miniature. And top-1 is worse than dense: each token then gets a 32-wide expert instead of a 64-wide MLP, and with a single gate there is no gate gradient to teach the router. Held-out expert usage stayed within a few percent of even in every top-2 run. Switching the balancing loss off was measured too: routing goes lopsided rather than collapsing (44/9/8/40% with 4 experts; with 8, two experts end at 0% and 3%), the loss is unchanged at 4 experts and about 0.007 worse at 8. At this scale the balancing term is cheap insurance rather than a necessity.

What the experts learned

With MICROGPT_EXPERT_STATS=1 a run prints which expert each input character and each position is routed to on the held-out names. For the 8-experts-of-64 model the split is by character class, and it is sharp:

ExpertTakes (share of that character's positions)
6every name start (100% of BOS, 83% of position 1) and initial consonants: j 87%, b 64%, p 64%, f 56%, k 53%, c 50%
1a 94%
3e 88%, y 81%, i 78%
2o 68%, some z, k, m

0v 88%, d 61%, g 54%, t 54%
4second positions and u: the vowel right after an initial consonant

This is why the mixture helps. In a character model, the current character largely fixes the shape of the next-character distribution: after a vowel a consonant is likely, after q comes u, after a name's first consonant comes a vowel. A single 64-wide MLP has to encode all of those regimes in one set of weights; eight specialised ones each encode a few, and the router, reading the hidden state, has learned to recognise the regime. The router was never told about characters. It found this split because sending like tokens to the same expert lowered the loss, and the balancing term kept it from parking everything on one or two experts.

Stacking both capacity gains was tried too: 2 layers, 32-dim, 8 experts of 64, top-2 (76,480 parameters) reaches 2.024 at 64 x 4 in 17.8 s, against 2.026 in 7.5 s for the dense 2-layer 32-dim model. At 640k names the gains barely stack; the model is no longer the limit, the data is.

Expert usage is printed per layer at the end of every mixture-of-experts run. Build with MICROGPT_N_EXPERTS=8 MICROGPT_TOP_K=2 MICROGPT_HIDDEN=64 cargo build --release.

07 · Recommendations

What to run

Want to see experts specialise: MICROGPT_N_EXPERTS=8 MICROGPT_TOP_K=2 MICROGPT_HIDDEN=64 cargo build --release, then run with MICROGPT_EXPERT_STATS=1 to print the routing tables in section 06.

Want under a second of training: the 32-dim single layer at 6700 x 16 on 4 threads reaches 2.107 in 0.8 s, better than the gist's shape gets in any amount of time at this recipe.

Want the lowest loss: the 2-layer 32-dim model at 64 names per step, 4 threads: 2.026 in 7.5 s. Adding 8 experts of 64 to it reaches 2.024 in 17.8 s, so the extra capacity is not worth its time at this data size. Want the best loss per second: a 16-dim layer with 8 experts of 64, top-2, at 64 x 4: 2.049 in 5.6 s.

Leave the learning rate at 0.01 for any batched run; lower it to 0.005 only for the one-name recipe. Use 6 threads instead of 4 when nothing else is running.

Not worth pursuing

  • FP16, FP8, FP4. The whole model is 16 to 100 KB and lives in L1 cache, so the bandwidth these formats save is not the bottleneck; fp16 arithmetic would at best halve the compute part of a step (a 1.6x ceiling at 16 x 4), needs nightly Rust, and underflows Adam's second moment outright. FP8 and FP4 have no CPU arithmetic at all.
  • More than 8 threads, or batches above 128. Both measured worse.

Next real speed-up

The position-batched matmuls predicted here were implemented afterwards and delivered 1.45x on the kernels (see the table in section 01), less than the 1.5 to 2.5x guessed, and the guess about why was wrong: the gain came from the forward pass, not from gradient-buffer traffic. The reduce-and-Adam fusion suggested next was then also done (6-8% at 4 to 8 threads). Instrumenting the main thread's phases at 4 threads shows what remains of a 44 µs step: about 33 µs of compute, 3 to 4 µs of update, and 5 to 6 µs waiting at the two barriers. The barrier waits are load imbalance: 16 names of 2 to 16 characters are claimed dynamically by 4 threads, so the step ends when the last claimed name does. Claiming longest names first would trim that; beyond it, the attention loop could be vectorised over heads.

08 · Method

How the numbers were taken

  • Held-out loss is the mean cross-entropy over the last 1,000 names of the seed-42 shuffle, which are never trained on. Every configuration is scored on the same names. Training-batch losses are not compared across batch sizes.
  • Timing is training wall-clock only, and every number on this page was re-measured with the final code (commit history in the repo records the intermediate builds). Loss logging snapshots the parameters (16 KB) during training and evaluates them afterwards; an earlier inline evaluator slowed the 8-thread run by 75% by disturbing the worker scheduling and was replaced.
  • Correctness rests on an f64 finite-difference gradient check over a few hundred parameters (max relative error under 1e-4, run for all four model shapes and for the mixture-of-experts build), an incremental-versus-full forward equivalence test for the KV cache, and a check that 1-thread and 3-thread training produce the same parameters to 1e-9.
  • Machine: Apple M1 Pro, 8 performance + 2 efficiency cores, Rust 1.97 stable. The Python baseline is CPython 3 on the same laptop. The RNG is xoshiro256** and does not reproduce Python's stream, so exact losses differ from the gist.
  • Code: src/main.rs (about 1,600 lines including tests), build.rs for the model shape and expert configuration, docs/loss-curves.html and docs/loss-logs/ for the recorded runs.
