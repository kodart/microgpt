microgpt · Rust port · Apple M1 Pro · September 2026

The gist's GPT, 9,000x faster, and what that speed let us learn

Karpathy's microgpt.py trains a 4,192-parameter character-level GPT on 32,033 names with a scalar autograd engine. This port keeps the maths and drops the engine. The wall-clock savings were then spent on the sweeps below: batch size, threads, learning rate, seeds and model size, each judged on the same 1,000 held-out names.

01 · What changed

Same maths, no graph

The Python version allocates a Value node for every add and multiply and walks that graph backwards; that is essentially all of its runtime. The port derives the backward pass by hand for each op and runs it as loops over flat f32 buffers. Everything else is bookkeeping around that. Each row below was measured in isolation, in the order it was added.

ChangeWhat it doesMeasured effect
Hand-derived vector backwardExplicit backward kernels for embedding, rmsnorm, fused QKV, causal softmax attention, output projection, ReLU MLP, LM head and cross-entropy. Parameters, gradients and Adam moments are four flat vectors.109 s → 20 ms
Fixed-size activation arraysActivations and backward scratch are inline arrays in one boxed struct instead of separate heap Vecs. With Vecs reached through a reference, LLVM cannot prove the buffers are disjoint and emits ~25% fewer vector ops plus spills. This cost a day's worth of bisecting to find.15.9 → 12.5 µs per name
Vector FMA kernelsEvery accumulate uses mul_add; the dot product keeps four independent lanes. Rust neither contracts a*b+c nor reassociates float sums, so the hot loop had 602 multiplies and 600 adds and now has 568 fused multiply-adds. A serial FMA chain was slower (latency-bound); the lanes are what matters.12.2 → 10.6 µs per name
Position-batched linear layersForward and backward apply each linear layer to all positions of a name at once. The weight gradient becomes dW += dY^T X summed over positions with 16-float chunks of each output row held in registers, so dW is touched once per name instead of once per position.10.6 → 9.1 µs per name
Forward against transposed weightsA profile (never-inlined kernels under sample) showed the forward's one-dot-per-output form taking 45% of the time for a third of the FLOPs: a 16-element dot is mostly horizontal-reduction overhead. The forward now reads a transposed copy of the weights, refreshed after each Adam step in about a microsecond, and computes each output row as register-accumulated y += x[j]·Wt[j] updates, the same shape as the backward matmuls. Two earlier guesses, a fast exp and a 4-row tiled dot, measured no gain and a loss respectively; the profile was what found this.9.1 → 7.3 µs per name
Minibatches over a worker poolWorkers claim names from an atomic counter and accumulate into private gradient buffers; the main thread reduces, runs Adam and publishes the next step on an atomic epoch. Spin-then-yield waits, because a step is tens of microseconds and channel wake-ups cost more than the compute.2.8x at 4 threads, 5x at 8
Reduce-and-Adam fused into the workersThe per-step tail (sum the gradient buffers, Adam, transpose) ran on the main thread while the others spun. Each worker now does it for its own parameter slice behind a second barrier, on lock-free shared buffers whose soundness rests on the barriers. Instrumenting the phases was essential: a first version was 3x slower per element than the old serial tail because its chunked reduce ran the vector sqrt/div latency-bound and its index-table scatter defeated the store pipeline; flat loops and a strided transpose fixed both.46.8 → 43.8 µs per step at 4 threads; 63 → 58 at 8
Build flagsFat LTO, one codegen unit, panic=abort, target-cpu=native.baseline

Per-name costs are single-thread, 32 names per step; the port now trains the gist's model at about 7.3 µs per name, against roughly 100 ms per name in Python. The KV cache for sampling comes for free: the stored K/V rows of the activation buffer are the cache, and generation runs one new position against them. The 4-thread speed-up fell from 3.2x to 2.6x as the kernels got faster, because the per-step synchronisation cost did not shrink with them.

02 · Threads

Scaling flattens early, and eight threads are fragile

Time per optimizer step, 32 names per step

Quiet-machine measurements, 20000 steps, taken before the two matmul changes above (the kernels are now 1.45x faster at one thread, so absolute values are lower; the shape of the curve, and the fixed synchronisation cost that bends it, are unchanged). Ten-thread runs are omitted: on this 8-performance + 2-efficiency-core CPU they were 1.6 to 3x slower than eight and never repeatable.

Table view

A 32-name step is now about 30 µs of arithmetic on eight cores; the barrier plus reduce-and-Adam is another 25 to 30 µs and does not shrink with threads. That fixed cost is why the curve bends. It is also why eight threads are a bad default: with all performance cores busy, any other process (a browser, or macOS's Gatekeeper scanning freshly built binaries, which happened repeatedly during this work) turned 1.5 s runs into 3 to 8 s, while 4-thread runs stayed at 1.1 s throughout. Six threads are about 20% faster than four on a quiet machine and are worth passing explicitly.

03 · Batch size

Larger batches learn more per name, up to 64

Table view

Two results that went against the usual intuition. At equal data, larger batches win: the one-name-per-step recipe updates most often but its gradients are so noisy that the extra updates are worth less than the noise costs, and the trend only flattens at 64. And larger batches do not want a larger learning rate: 0.01 is already the optimum for 16- and 64-name batches, with every higher rate strictly worse. The batched runs are noise-limited, not step-limited. The one recipe that does want a change is the gist's own, which improves from 2.223 to 2.203 at a rate of 0.005.

Four seeds of the default run end at 2.120, 2.122, 2.123 and 2.130. Differences under about 0.01 in any table here are noise; the batch-size and model-size effects are several times that.

05 · Model size

Capacity was the real limit

Table view

Every larger shape beats the gist's model at every budget, including the one-second budget where the bigger models get a fraction of the steps. The 32-dimensional single layer is the best buy up to a few seconds and then saturates near 2.055; the 2-layer 32-dimensional model keeps improving and wins once ten seconds are affordable. 06 · Recommendations

What to run

Want a second of training: the 32-dim single layer at 6700 x 16 on 4 threads reaches 2.104 in 1.1 s, better than the gist's shape gets in any amount of time at this recipe.

Want the lowest loss: the 2-layer 32-dim model at 64 names per step, 4 threads. 2.035 in 11 s, and still improving with more steps.

Leave the learning rate at 0.01 for any batched run; lower it to 0.005 only for the one-name recipe. Use 6 threads instead of 4 when nothing else is running.

Not worth pursuing

  • FP16, FP8, FP4. The whole model is 16 to 100 KB and lives in L1 cache, so the bandwidth these formats save is not the bottleneck; fp16 arithmetic would at best halve the compute part of a step (a 1.6x ceiling at 16 x 4), needs nightly Rust, and underflows Adam's second moment outright. FP8 and FP4 have no CPU arithmetic at all.
  • More than 8 threads, or batches above 128. Both measured worse.

Next real speed-up

The position-batched matmuls predicted here were implemented afterwards and delivered 1.45x on the kernels (see the table in section 01), less than the 1.5 to 2.5x guessed, and the guess about why was wrong: the gain came from the forward pass, not from gradient-buffer traffic. The reduce-and-Adam fusion suggested next was then also done (6-8% at 4 to 8 threads). Instrumenting the main thread's phases at 4 threads shows what remains of a 44 µs step: about 33 µs of compute, 3 to 4 µs of update, and 5 to 6 µs waiting at the two barriers. The barrier waits are load imbalance: 16 names of 2 to 16 characters are claimed dynamically by 4 threads, so the step ends when the last claimed name does. Claiming longest names first would trim that; beyond it, the attention loop could be vectorised over heads.

07 · Method

How the numbers were taken

  • Held-out loss is the mean cross-entropy over the last 1,000 names of the seed-42 shuffle, which are never trained on. Every configuration is scored on the same names. Training-batch losses are not compared across batch sizes.
  • Timing is training wall-clock only. Loss logging snapshots the parameters (16 KB) during training and evaluates them afterwards; an earlier inline evaluator slowed the 8-thread run by 75% by disturbing the worker scheduling and was replaced.
  • Correctness rests on an f64 finite-difference gradient check over a few hundred parameters (max relative error under 1e-4, run for all four model shapes), an incremental-versus-full forward equivalence test for the KV cache, and a check that 1-thread and 3-thread training produce the same parameters to 1e-9.
  • Machine: Apple M1 Pro, 8 performance + 2 efficiency cores, Rust 1.97 stable. The Python baseline is CPython 3 on the same laptop. The RNG is xoshiro256** and does not reproduce Python's stream, so exact losses differ from the gist.
  • Code: src/main.rs (about 1,200 lines including tests), build.rs for the model shape, docs/loss-curves.html and docs/loss-logs/ for the recorded runs.
